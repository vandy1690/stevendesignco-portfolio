## The trade it makes

Pinning a card to the viewport buys focus and costs flexibility. The card must
fit the window, so copy length, image ratio and viewport height all become
layout constraints rather than content decisions.

Everything below follows from that.

## Height budget

The stage holds a heading, the card and the dot row. The card is capped at the
viewport height minus what sits above it. Change any part and the others must be
re-measured.

Two failures came from this. Taking twenty pixels from the card cap to fit the
dot row pushed the two longest cards over their limit and dropped several common
phone sizes into the fallback list. And the card art, being the only flexible
item, silently absorbed every shortfall: at 402 by 660 it rendered 61 pixels
tall against an intended 199, a sliver instead of a picture.

The art is now pinned to its ratio and the body gives up the space instead.

## How it degrades

The deck becomes a plain list in four cases, described in
[Degradation](/docs/practices/degradation/). The important one is the runtime
check: after layout, the page measures whether a card actually fits and drops to
the list if not. Media queries only know the viewport; they do not know that
this visitor's text is larger or this card's copy is longer.

## Keyboard access

Only the face-on card is visible, so only one of five links is reachable by Tab.
A hidden list inside the section carries all five, revealed when focused. Without
it, a keyboard user could reach exactly one case study from the home page.

## Checks before you ship

1. Scroll through all five. Each card is fully visible with its call to action.
2. At 402 by 660 and 402 by 874, the deck runs and the art holds its ratio.
3. Below 600 pixels tall, the list appears instead.
4. Tab into the section. The hidden jump list appears and reaches every case study.
